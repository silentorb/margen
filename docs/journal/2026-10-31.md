The current algorithm employed by margen is a system I've refined over a decade of experimentation.  It's the best solution I've come up with so far, but I think I see a way to make things better.

One of the challenges of world generation is seamlessly transitioning from high-level design to detailed implementation, from low fidelity to high fidelity.

The classic grid system for world generation is a right angle grid, but margen uses a hex grid because that leads to more organic topology, handles slopes more seamlessly, and doesn't have the diagonal issues of right angles.

However, the hex grid also has downsides:
1. It only uses hexes horizontally, there is no practical way to have hexes along all three axis.  The cells are effectively cylinders.
2. It can't be cleanly subdivided.  Larger hex cells can be composed of smaller cells, but the larger cells have jagged boundaries.
3. It is a little more complicated.  Not a show stopper but worth adding to the list.

Beyond that, there is a larger issue with any form of grid: it limits free form geometry.  The main reason I went with hex grids is because it provides more flexible geometry, while sticking closely to the original grid.

But sticking close to the original grid is an extreme limiter.

Some previous iterations of world generation solutions I created were free form, using a dynamic mesh that could support any kind of angles.  The problem I ran into was everything was relative, and the simplest algorithms required extremely complex neighborhood analysis and traversal.  Grids provide a global, absolute way for various records in the map to relate to each other, and a straightforward means for algorithms to traverse those records.

Yesterday while I was on a walk through a nearby forest and considering this problem and the natural geometry surrounding me, it hit me, I'm treating the grid as too high fidelity.  Any grid is a restriction at high fidelity.

Where grids are most useful are at low fidelity, higher-level design, especially if the grid is treated as an approximation requiring heurstics to interpret.

With that in mind, the benefits of a hex grid become less important.  Still useful, but no longer essential for organic topology.

With a hex grid no longer essential for the desired outcome, a right angle grid becomes a viable option again.  In particular, an octree, so the grid can seamlessly scale along all three axis.  That scaling will help translate between low and high fidelity, though high fidelity still shouldn't be constrained by the grid.  The grid will always be an approximation, but scaling will help mitigate the gap between the approximation and the final geometry.

It's important to note that the current margen flow does *not* have a free form state, and introducing one will require more steps of translation, specifically more steps translating between grid form free form.

Any translation should be subdivided into a series of smaller intermediate translations, and the more complex the translation, the more intermediate steps are required.

This proposed new direction will make the translation more complicated, and thus will need more steps.  The generation flow can't suddenly jump from a grid data structure to a free form data structure.

However, I'm not yet entirely sure what those intermediate steps will be yet.  That is a major unknown and that area of the scheme may contain fundamental flaws I am not aware of yet which will require me to back up.
